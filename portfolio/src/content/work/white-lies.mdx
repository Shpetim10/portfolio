<Chapter id="problem">

Every chess player grows up hearing that White has the edge because White moves first. It is one of the most repeated claims in the game, but most of the numbers quoted for it come from small or hand-picked game collections, and many of them confuse winning more often with scoring more points.

Built with three teammates at Holberton School Albania, White Lies tests the claim against a whole month of real play. It asks two questions. Does White earn more than half the points? And after 1.e4 or 1.d4, does Black's very first reply go with different results? The aim was to answer both with the kind of evidence a statistician would sign off on: reproducible, honest about its uncertainty, and open for anyone to check.

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="constraints">

The source is the Lichess monthly archive: 97,512,351 rated games for March 2025, in a single compressed file of about 31.8 GB. That is far too much to download and keep, yet the sample had to cover every day of the month, not just the first few hours of the file.

The data is observational. Players choose their openings, and ratings differ between opponents, so any difference in results can come from who is playing rather than from the move itself. Some groups are scarce, such as classical games and the highest rating bands, and the method had to make up for that without biasing the totals. Every number the site shows also had to be traceable to a recorded, checked dataset.

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="architecture">

A Python pipeline in four stages. A streaming collector reads the archive straight from Lichess, samples and filters games as the bytes arrive, and writes a Parquet dataset together with a manifest that records exactly what happened. The analysis validates that dataset, weights each game for how it was sampled, and runs the two hypothesis tests, H1 for White's score and H2 for Black's first reply, followed by robustness and sensitivity checks. Reports, 11 scientific figures and a static research website are generated from those results, and GitHub Actions publishes the site to GitHub Pages.

<Chapter id="decisions">

### Stream the month, never store it

I reworked the collector so the archive is decompressed as it downloads and only the sampled games are written. The whole file is always read, with no early stop, so the sample spans all 31 days. The March run read 31.8 GB of compressed data in under four hours.

### Sample by game ID, so every run agrees

A game is kept when a hash of its Lichess ID falls under the sampling rate, so the same game is always kept or always dropped, on any machine and in any run. The check happens right after a game's headers are read, before its moves are parsed, so skipping a game costs almost nothing. Scarce groups are sampled at 10% instead of 1% (classical games and the 2200 and 2500 rating bands), and each game carries its sampling rate so the totals can be weighted back to the real population.

### Keep the resignations

Lichess files do not mark resignations, so the earlier dataset had dropped those games, which inflated the draw rate and skewed the openings. The new collector keeps them and flags them instead: a decisive game that ended normally without checkmate. Every game that is rejected is counted by rule in the manifest, alongside the bytes read, the code version and a checksum of the output, and a cut-off download is marked incomplete rather than passed off as finished. I wrote offline tests for the collector so all of this can be checked without downloading the archive.

### Score, not win rate

The study measures White's score, one point for a win and half a point for a draw, not how often White wins. Confidence intervals come from 10,000 bootstrap replicates, results across many groups are corrected for multiple testing, and the findings are rerun on restricted samples, such as games between players within 25 Elo of each other, to see whether they still hold.

<Chapter id="results">

White scores 51.78% (95% CI 51.65–51.90%) across 836,070 sampled games, 1.78 points per 100 games above an even split. The edge holds in blitz, rapid and classical alike, and in all nine rating bands, from beginners to players rated 2500 and above. It survives the checks: among players rated within 25 Elo of each other, White still scores 51.82%. In context, moving first is worth about 2 points per 100 games, while a rating edge of 100 Elo or more is worth about 17.

Black's first reply is a subtler story. In 14 of the 35 groups that could be tested, replies went with clearly different scores, but when the players' ratings are closely matched only 1 group in 28 keeps that difference. So the study reports an association and deliberately does not rank one defence above another.

<Chapter id="reflection">

The study covers one month, one platform and selected rated game types, and it does not prove that moving first causes the difference. The natural next steps are the ones it names as out of scope: collecting a full year, adding master-level games and lining the openings up fully with their ECO codes. The method was built to make that easy, since the collector runs the same way on any month and every result traces back to a recorded dataset.

</Chapter>
