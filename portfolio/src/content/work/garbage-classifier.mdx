<Chapter id="problem">

Sorting waste correctly is essential for recycling, yet items often end up in the wrong bin. The project explored whether a neural network could recognize the type of waste from a single photo, a building block for smarter sorting and recycling guidance and tips.

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="constraints">

An image dataset from Kaggle with uneven image quality, limited computing resources that made training slow, and a constant risk of overfitting on a modest dataset.

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="architecture">

A Python and TensorFlow pipeline: images are cleaned and preprocessed, then passed to a convolutional network pretrained on a large image collection, with a new classification head trained for the waste categories. Candidate models were compared on held-out data.

<Chapter id="decisions">

Transfer learning instead of training a network from scratch. A pretrained backbone reached higher accuracy with far less data and compute, which suited our limited resources.

Cleaning the data before training. Removing or fixing low-quality images meant the model learned from reliable examples.

Adjusting the model during training. When validation results showed signs of overfitting, I tuned the training setup rather than simply training longer.

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="results">

My transfer-learning model achieved the highest accuracy of the team's candidates and was selected as the final model. That model reached ±97% accuracy in test dataset. We created a small web app which is helpful for everyday use, as an AI assistant for helping classifying garbage and acting as an environment-friendly citizen.

<Chapter id="reflection">

I would track experiments systematically and report per-class precision and recall, not only overall accuracy. I would keep transfer learning as my default starting point for small image datasets.
