<Chapter id="problem">

Writing accurate, engaging history lessons in two languages is slow, and AI can speed it up, but language models confidently invent facts. For an education product, one invented date destroys trust. HERISAGA needed a way to use AI for drafting while keeping every claim traceable to a real source.

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="constraints">

Every lesson must stay traceable to historical evidence. Machine-generated drafts always require human review. Content is bilingual, and once an episode is published it must never change under a learner mid-session.

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="architecture">

The foundation is a source-to-facts pipeline, written in Python and FastAPI. It takes each historical source, splits it into chunks that stay tied to their source, and indexes them as vector embeddings in PostgreSQL with pgvector. For a topic it retrieves chunks with dense and lexical search, reranks them with Voyage, and checks that the evidence clears a threshold before anything is extracted. An LLM, called through the OpenAI SDK, then extracts structured facts from the strongest chunks, each citing the section and pages it came from. People review those facts in the Episode Studio, a FastAPI, Jinja2 and HTMX web app, and the passages, exercises and translations are drafted from the approved ones, each carrying the grounding facts behind it. Editors refine the bilingual episode in Directus and approve it there, and publishing records the exact revision that goes out as immutable, schema-validated content for the CDN.

<Chapter id="decisions">

Do the reading in a pipeline, and start every episode from facts. The source-to-facts pipeline turns long historical sources into something an editor can work with straight away: structured facts, each saying who did what to whom and when, tagged with a reliability tier, citing the section and pages it came from, and held in English and Albanian from the start. Editors begin with a cited, bilingual list of claims instead of a blank page, which makes an episode both faster to build and better grounded.

Retrieve before generating. The model never writes from memory. The Retrieval console shows what it would be working from: the chunks found, how each one scored, whether the evidence clears a threshold, and what the extraction will cost. Nothing runs until that cost is confirmed.

Review facts, not only finished text. A reviewer reads each extracted claim in English and Albanian beside the highlighted passage it came from, and approves, rejects, edits or skips it from the keyboard, one at a time or all at once. Checking a claim against its cited source is quicker than researching it from scratch. An automated evidence check, JEV, scores how well a passage supports a claim, but it never approves anything on its own. Exercises then list the grounding facts behind them, so evidence travels with the content.

<Chapter id="reflection">

I would run one real source through the complete workflow, from retrieval to a learner playing it, earlier, and tune retrieval quality against that real example. I would keep evidence-first drafting, because in education, trust is the product.
